One. What this is.

There is a Hebrew song that people sing before a journey. This is its name. [he: תְּפִלַּת הַדֶּרֶךְ.] Once more, slowly. [he: תְּפִלַּת. הַדֶּרֶךְ.] It means the prayer of the road — in your own language, [ar: صَلاةُ الطَّريق].

[he: תְּפִלַּת הַדֶּרֶךְ]

[[PAUSE:4.0]]

[he: תְּפִלַּת הַדֶּרֶךְ]

Say it with me. You hear it, then there is a silence that belongs to you, then you hear it again. That silence is the whole method — do not skip it, and do not be shy in it.

The first lesson is already hiding in that name, and it is Arabic grammar. [he: תְּפִלַּת הַדֶּרֶךְ] is a construct chain — prayer-of the-road. It is your [ar: إِضافَة], joint for joint. Hebrew even clips the first word short to mark the join, exactly the way you would.

And a small word is glued to the front of the second half — [he: הַ]. That is the definite article, and it is your [ar: أَلْ]. Different origin, identical job, identical position. Hebrew says [he: הַדֶּרֶךְ] where you say [ar: الطَّريق].

How this lesson works. Every Hebrew word is spoken by a Hebrew voice, and its Arabic cousin right after it. Every line is built from its end backwards, one piece at a time. After each piece comes a real silence — that one is yours.

Two. The decoder ring.

Before any of the prayer, take a key, because it turns almost every lock in this text.

When Hebrew and Arabic split apart, the consonants did not scatter randomly. They drifted in regular, predictable pairs. Learn six of them and you can decode Hebrew you have never seen.

The first and most famous. This Hebrew letter — [he: שִׁין] — answers to your plain [ar: سِين]. So this Hebrew word, [he: שָׁלוֹם], is your [ar: سَلامٌ]. Spell it in Hebrew: [he: שִׁין, לָמֶד, מֵם.] Now in Arabic: [ar: سِين، لام، مِيم.] The same word, the same greeting, in both directions, to this day.

[he: שָׁלוֹם]

[[PAUSE:3.0]]

[he: שָׁלוֹם]

Second pair. The Hebrew letter [he: צָדִי] answers to your [ar: صاد]. This Hebrew word, [he: צָעַד], means to step or to march. Swap [he: צָדִי] for [ar: صاد] and you get [ar: صَعِدَ] — which in your mouth means to climb. One root, one stepping motion; Arabic simply aimed it upward.

Third pair, and this one is lovely because Arabic kept a sound Hebrew lost. The Hebrew letter [he: זַיִן] answers to your [ar: ذال]. This word, [he: אָחַז], means to seize, to take hold of. Swap the [he: זַיִן] for a [ar: ذال] and there is [ar: أَخَذَ]. You still pronounce the tooth in it; Hebrew flattened it to a z.

Fourth. The Hebrew letter [he: חֵית] is your [ar: حاء], the deep one from the throat. Hear the word for life: [he: חַיִּים] — [ar: حَياةٌ.] Most Israelis today say that letter further back, closer to your [ar: خاء], so that is what you will hear here. But Iraqi and Yemenite Jews always said it exactly as you do, and theirs is the older sound.

Fifth, same story. The Hebrew letter [he: עַיִן] is your [ar: عَين]. Listen: [he: עוֹלָם] — [ar: عالَمٌ.] World, in both. Modern Israeli speech has dropped that letter almost entirely, so you will barely hear it. Mizrahi communities never dropped it — yours is the conservative pronunciation, not theirs.

And sixth. The Hebrew letter [he: קוֹף] answers to your [ar: قاف]. Listen: [he: קוֹל] — [ar: قَولٌ.] Hebrew kept the sound coming out; Arabic kept what was said.

There is the whole key on one card. Hear the six straight through, Hebrew then Arabic. [he: שִׁין] — [ar: سِين.] [he: צָדִי] — [ar: صاد.] [he: זַיִן] — [ar: ذال.] [he: חֵית] — [ar: حاء.] [he: עַיִן] — [ar: عَين.] [he: קוֹף] — [ar: قاف.] Keep those in your hand.

Three. Reading the Arabic transcription.

The middle line on every card is Hebrew written in Arabic letters, so that you can read it. Five borrowed letters, and then we begin.

For a v, the Persian letter on this card — your [ar: فاء] carrying three dots instead of one. It matters, because the little word for "and" that opens almost every line of this prayer is that sound.

For a hard grams, a [ar: كاف] with a second stroke. Always hard — never your [ar: جيم].

For p, a [ar: باء] with three dots. Arabic lost that sound; Hebrew kept it.

Then the strangest-looking one. The Hebrew letter [he: צָדִי] is a single sound, ts, and Arabic has no letter for it, so I write a [ar: تاء] followed by a [ar: سِين]. Do not say those separately — it is one sound.

And the last I write with your own [ar: خاء]. One more rule before we start, the most useful in the language: Hebrew stress falls on the last syllable, nearly always. Push every word to its end.

Four. Line one.

Here is the whole first line once, so you know where we are going. [he: יְהִי רָצוֹן מִלְּפָנֶיךָ, אֲדוֹנָי אֱלֹהֵינוּ וֵאלֹהֵי אֲבוֹתֵינוּ.] Now we take it apart.

The second word is built on [he: צָדִי]. Run it through the ring — [he: צָדִי] becomes [ar: صاد] — and out falls your own word. Listen to the pair: [he: רָצוֹן] — [ar: رِضْوانٌ.] So the phrase that opens this prayer, and a thousand others, means let there be good-pleasure. It is your [ar: لِيَكُنْ رِضًا], word for word.

Five. Line two.

This is the line that gives the song its shape, because it says one word three times. [he: שֶׁתּוֹלִיכֵנוּ לְשָׁלוֹם, וְתַצְעִידֵנוּ לְשָׁלוֹם, וְתַדְרִיכֵנוּ לְשָׁלוֹם.]

Six. Line three.

This line holds my favourite buried word in the whole text. [he: וְתַגִּיעֵנוּ לִמְחוֹז חֶפְצֵנוּ, לְחַיִּים וּלְשִׂמְחָה וּלְשָׁלוֹם.]

This word — [he: מְחוֹז] — is usually translated destination, but it means a harbour, a haven, a place you put in at. And it comes from [he: אָחַז], to seize, which you now know is [ar: أَخَذَ]. A harbour is the place you take hold of. In your letters, its shape would be [ar: مَأخَذٌ].

Then three things are asked for. Life — [he: חַיִּים] — [ar: حَياةٌ.] Peace, which you have. And in the middle, joy: [he: שִׂמְחָה], spelled [he: שִׂין, מֵם, חֵית] — in your letters [ar: سِين، مِيم، حاء] — which gives [ar: سَماحةٌ]: generosity, openness, an easy spirit. Ten. Line seven.

The longest line, and the richest — three words in a row are all yours. [he: וְתִתְּנֵנִי לְחֵן וּלְחֶסֶד וּלְרַחֲמִים, בְּעֵינֶיךָ וּבְעֵינֵי כָל רוֹאֵינוּ.]

And the third is the jewel of the whole prayer. [he: רַחֲמִים], mercy — [he: רֵישׁ, חֵית, מֵם], your [ar: راء، حاء، مِيم]. Listen: [he: רַחֲמִים] — [ar: رَحْمةٌ.] And of course [ar: الرَّحمٰنُ الرَّحيم].

Eleven. Line eight.

Three words, and every one of them is yours. [he: וְתִשְׁמַע קוֹל תַּחֲנוּנֵינוּ.]

[he: תִּשְׁמַע] is [he: שִׁין, מֵם, עַיִן] — through the ring, [ar: سِين، مِيم، عَين]: [ar: سَمِعَ.] Hear the pair: [he: תִּשְׁמַע] — [ar: تَسمَعُ.] And it is not only the root, it is the conjugation — the Hebrew prefix that marks you is [he: תָּו], which is your [ar: تاء]. The same word, the same tense, the same prefix. Nothing separates them at all.

Then [he: קוֹל], a voice — [ar: قَولٌ.] And [he: תַּחֲנוּן], supplication, which comes from that same [he: חֵן] we just met. Here is the beautiful part: Hebrew builds this shape, [he: תַּחֲנוּן], and Arabic builds the identical shape from the identical root and calls it [ar: تَحَنُّنٌ]. Not merely the same root — the same pattern on the same root.

Twelve. Line nine.

Here the prayer gives its reason. [he: כִּי אֵל שׁוֹמֵעַ תְּפִלָּה וְתַחֲנוּן אָתָּה.]

Put two words of that line side by side — [he: שׁוֹמֵעַ], hearing, and [he: אֵל], God. God hears. In Hebrew, that is [he: יִשְׁמָעֵאל]. Which is the name [ar: إِسماعيل]. The name of Ismail is not a decorative sound. It is a whole sentence, and it is this sentence — the one sitting in the ninth line of this prayer.

One word in that line has no Arabic cousin: [he: תְּפִלָּה], prayer itself. But the traffic runs the other way, because your own word for prayer is a borrowing from this family. [ar: صَلاةٌ] came into Arabic from the Aramaic [he: צְלוֹתָא], built on a root meaning to bow. When you say your own word for prayer, you are already speaking a cousin language.

And the last word: [he: אָתָּה], meaning you. Yours is [ar: أَنتَ]. They look different, until you learn that Hebrew swallowed the n. The older form was exactly yours, and the [he: נוּן] assimilated into the [he: תָּו] — which is why Hebrew still writes a small dot inside that letter to this day. That dot is a tombstone for your [ar: نون].

Fifteen. One last thing.

There is a road prayer in your own tradition too, and it belongs beside this one. [ar: اللَّهُمَّ إِنّا نَسأَلُكَ في سَفَرِنا هٰذا البِرَّ والتَّقوى.]

And a little further in. [ar: أَعوذُ بِكَ مِنْ وَعْثاءِ السَّفَرِ وكَآبةِ المَنظَرِ وسوءِ المُنقَلَب.]

Listen to what that one is afraid of. The hardship of the road. The gloom of what you might see. A bad homecoming. And the Hebrew one, centuries apart, is afraid of the enemy, the ambusher, the bandit, the wild animal.

Two texts, made by people who knew that the road was where you got killed, and who both decided the answer was to say something out loud before setting off. One asks for [he: שָׁלוֹם]. The other asks for [ar: سَلامٌ]. Which, as you now know, is not two words at all.

Safe travels.
