One. What Part One left out.

In Part One you learned which consonants match. That got you the words. It did not get you the machine — how Hebrew builds a verb, what the little one-letter words are doing, and why almost every verb in this prayer has the same hidden shape.

That is this lesson. And at the end we lay [ar: دُعاءُ السَّفَر] beside it, because that is where we are going.

But first, a second key. Part One's ring was for consonants. Here is the one for vowels, and it is a single rule.

Two. The vowel ring.

Say your word for peace and listen to the long vowel in the middle. [ar: سَلامٌ.] Now the Hebrew. [he: שָׁלוֹם.] Your [ar: ألِف] has turned into an o. That is not a coincidence in this one word — it is a sound law that ran through the entire language, and once you know it you can convert on the fly.

Again, with world. Yours: [ar: عالَمٌ.] Theirs: [he: עוֹלָם.] Same shift, same position.

And once more, with a word that was not in Part One. Tongue. Yours: [ar: لِسانٌ.] Theirs: [he: לָשׁוֹן.] Listen for it: [ar: لِسانٌ] — [he: לָשׁוֹן.]

One rule, and it unlocks a whole shelf of vocabulary. Whenever you meet a Hebrew word with an o where you expect an alif, you are probably looking at your own word.

Three. The showcase — the doer.

Now watch the vowel ring do real work. In Arabic, to turn a root into the one who does it, you use the shape [ar: فاعِل]. Hearing gives you [ar: سامِعٌ]. Apply the vowel ring — that long vowel turns into an o — and you get the Hebrew: [he: שׁוֹמֵעַ.] Listen to them next to each other. [ar: سامِعٌ] — [he: שׁוֹמֵעַ.]

That is not a similar word. It is the same word, with one regular vowel shift. And it is sitting in the last line of the prayer.

It works on anything. Writing: [ar: كاتِبٌ] — [he: כּוֹתֵב.] Guarding: [ar: حافِظٌ] — and Hebrew would build [he: חוֹפֵץ] on the very root we chased last time.

Four. The connectors — seven single letters.

Now the small words. Hebrew glues them onto the front of the word, exactly as you do — and they are almost all yours. The first one opens nearly every line of this prayer: [he: וְ] is your [ar: وَ].

[he: הַ] is your [ar: الْ]. You met this one in Part One: [he: הַדֶּרֶךְ] is [ar: الطَّريق].

[he: בְּ] is your [ar: بِ]. From the prayer: [he: בְּכָל] — [ar: بِكُلِّ.] Identical, letter and job.

[he: לְ] is your [ar: لِ]. From the prayer: [he: לְשָׁלוֹם] — [ar: لِسَلامٍ.]

[he: כְּ] is your [ar: كَ], the one you use for comparison.

[he: מִ] is your [ar: مِنْ], shortened and glued on. From the prayer: [he: מִכַּף] — [ar: مِنْ كَفِّ.]

And one that is not a prefix but a whole word: [he: כִּי], which opens line nine. It is your [ar: كَيْ].

Five. The conjugation — four letters you already know.

Here is the centre of the lesson. To say he hears, you hear, I hear, we hear, Arabic puts a letter on the front of the verb. So does Hebrew. And it is the same four letters, in the same jobs.

Let us build it on the root you know best — hearing.

Six. The endings — us, me, you.

Now the other end of the verb. Hebrew hangs the object on the back, exactly as you do. Us is [he: ־נוּ] — your [ar: ـنا].

So take the verb for rescuing and hang it on: [he: תַּצִּילֵנוּ] — save-us. In your own mouth that shape is [ar: أَنْجِنا]. Verb, then the same little [ar: ـنا].

Seven. The secret shape of the whole prayer.

Now the insight this lesson exists for. Look again at the verbs of line two, and ask what they actually say. [he: שֶׁתּוֹלִיכֵנוּ] does not mean we walk. It means make us walk. Hebrew has a whole verb-shape whose only job is to add that make — and it is your [ar: أَفْعَلَ], the fourth form.

[he: וְתַצְעִידֵנוּ] — make-us-step. [he: וְתַדְרִיכֵנוּ] — make-us-tread-the-road.

[he: וְתַגִּיעֵנוּ] — make-us-arrive, which is your [ar: بَلِّغْنا]. And [he: וְתַצִּילֵנוּ] — literally make-us-slip-away, which is how both languages say rescue.

Look at what that means. This prayer never once says we travel. Every single verb is You make us travel. The traveller is not the subject of his own journey anywhere in this text — he is the object of somebody else's verb, five times in a row. That is the theology of the thing, hidden in the grammar rather than stated.

[he: שֶׁתּוֹלִיכֵנוּ לְשָׁלוֹם]

Eight. Two roots that split.

You liked the one where the two languages took opposite ends of a meaning. Here is another, and it is in line six. Hebrew [he: שָׁלַח] means to send. Your [ar: سِلاح] means a weapon. Same three consonants — and the bridge is still visible in Hebrew, because [he: שֶׁלַח] is also a Hebrew word for a missile, a thing thrown.

So the shared root meant the thing you send flying. Arabic kept the object in the hand. Hebrew kept the act of sending — and now uses it, in this prayer, for sending a blessing.

And one more, from line five. That long word for calamities is built on the root [he: פֶּרַע], which in Hebrew means hair left loose and unbound — and from there, unrestrained, then wild, then disaster. Your [ar: فَرْع] means a branch — and, in the older language, a full head of long hair.

One picture underneath both: something growing out freely in all directions. Arabic followed it up into the tree and got branches, subdivisions, secondary matters. Hebrew followed it into human behaviour and got chaos, then catastrophe.

Nine. Now the other prayer.

Now put your own road prayer next to it. [ar: اللَّهُمَّ إِنّا نَسْأَلُكَ في سَفَرِنا هٰذا البِرَّ والتَّقوى.] Listen to one word in it: [ar: نَسْأَلُكَ].

[ar: نَسْأَلُكَ] is a [ar: نون] on the front — we — and a [ar: ـكَ] on the back — you. Exactly the machine you just learned. Hebrew builds the same word the same way: [he: נִשְׁאַל], we-ask, with the same [he: נ] on the front.

Now the two lines that are doing the same job. Yours: [ar: أَعوذُ بِكَ مِنْ وَعْثاءِ السَّفَر.] Theirs: [he: וְתַצִּילֵנוּ מִכַּף כָּל אוֹיֵב.]

Both hang on the same little word. You say [ar: مِنْ]. They say [he: מִ]. Both are naming the danger of the road and asking to be taken out of it.

And the deepest one. Your prayer says [ar: اللَّهُمَّ اطْوِ لَنَا البُعْدَ وهَوِّنْ عَلَيْنَا السَّفَر] — fold up the distance for us, make the journey easy on us. That is a causative verb plus [ar: لَنا]. The Hebrew says [he: שֶׁתּוֹלִיכֵנוּ לְשָׁלוֹם] — a causative verb plus [he: ־נוּ].

Causative verb, plus us. In both. Neither traveller is asking for strength to go. Both are asking to be carried.

Hear them as pairs. Make it easy for us: [ar: هَوِّنْ عَلَيْنا] — [he: תּוֹלִיכֵנוּ.] Deliver us: [ar: أَنْجِنا] — [he: תַּצִּילֵנוּ.] Bring us there: [ar: بَلِّغْنا] — [he: תַּגִּיעֵנוּ.]

Two peoples, two prayers, the same three requests, built out of the same prefixes and the same endings, asking the same one thing: do not let me do this alone.
