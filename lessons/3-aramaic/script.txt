One. A voice you have not heard.

You asked how this word, from line three of the prayer, could possibly be related to your [ar: حِفْظ]. I gave you an answer built on a third language, and it did not land, because you were reading it instead of hearing it. So let us hear it.

This is that third language. [sy: חֲפַט.] That is Aramaic, and it is about to decide the argument. One honest note before we go on: there is no Aramaic text-to-speech voice anywhere, so what you just heard is a Hebrew voice — a different speaker, so you can tell them apart — reading Aramaic. That is fairer than it sounds, and in a moment you will see exactly why.

Two. Three sisters, and which is closest to which.

The family, in one breath. Hebrew and Aramaic are sisters — the Northwest Semitic branch. Arabic is their cousin, off a branch of its own. But the cousin is the one who kept the old sounds.

That last line is the paradox worth holding on to. [he: עִבְרִית] and [sy: אֲרָמִית] grew up next door to each other. [ar: العَرَبِيَّة] grew up further away. And yet it is the distant cousin — yours — that preserved the ancient sound system most completely. Which is precisely why your language is such a good key for opening Hebrew.

Hear the three of them name themselves. Aramaic: [sy: אֲרָמִית.] Hebrew: [he: עִבְרִית.] And yours: [ar: العَرَبِيَّة.]

And Aramaic is not a dead thing in a museum. It was the working language of the whole Near East for a thousand years — the empires ran on it, half the book of Daniel is in it, the Talmud is mostly in it. It is still spoken now, in Maaloula in Syria and among Assyrians and Chaldeans. When you hear [sy: אֲרָמִית], you are hearing a living tongue.

Three. The twist you did not expect.

Here is the part that should surprise you. Look at the letters Hebrew is written in — the square ones on these cards. Those are not originally Hebrew letters. Old Hebrew used a different, angular script; the square letters were borrowed from Aramaic during the Babylonian exile, and never given back.

And your own script? The Arabic alphabet descends from Nabataean, which is a form of Aramaic. So the letters you write, and the letters Hebrew writes, are two children of the same Aramaic parent that went in opposite directions and stopped recognising each other. Which is also why using a Hebrew voice to read Aramaic is not a cheat: that alphabet is Aramaic's alphabet.

Four. Why the third witness matters.

Now the actual problem. In the prayer lesson I told you Hebrew [he: צָדִי] answers to your [ar: صاد]. That was true but incomplete. Hebrew has one letter where you have three — [ar: صاد], [ar: ضاد], [ar: ظاء] — because Hebrew merged three ancient sounds into one and Arabic kept them apart.

The same thing happened with [he: חֵית], which covers both your [ar: حاء] and your [ar: خاء]. So when you meet a Hebrew word spelled with those letters, you cannot tell from the Hebrew alone which Arabic letter it started as. The Hebrew has thrown the information away.

And here is why the third sister rescues you. Aramaic also merged those sounds — but it merged them differently. Where Hebrew wrote everything as one letter, Aramaic split them three ways. So Aramaic still carries the information Hebrew lost. Put the two together and the original is recoverable.

So there is the rule, and it is worth hearing rather than reading. If Aramaic shows the same letter Hebrew shows — [sy: צָדֵי] — the original was your [ar: صاد]. If Aramaic shows [sy: עַיִן], the original was your [ar: ضاد]. And if Aramaic shows [sy: טֵית], the original was your [ar: ظاء].

Six. Now the argument.

Back to your word. [he: חֶפְצֵנוּ.] Its root is [he: חֵית, פֵּא, צָדִי]. From the Hebrew alone, that could have started as almost anything — two possibilities for the first letter, three for the last. Six candidate roots in Arabic, and no way to choose.

So we ask the third sister. Aramaic has this root, and it is [sy: חֲפַט.] Listen to the last letter. [sy: חֲפַט.] That is a [sy: טֵית].

And you just learned what a [sy: טֵית] means: the original was your [ar: ظاء]. Not [ar: صاد], not [ar: ضاد]. Which leaves exactly one Arabic root standing — [ar: حِفْظ]. That is not a guess from meaning. The sound law closed the case before meaning was ever consulted.

Seven. But what did it MEAN.

Now the harder half. [he: חֵפֶץ] means desire, delight, the thing you want. [ar: حِفْظ] means guarding, keeping, memorising. Put those two side by side with nothing in between and the connection looks like wishful thinking. And neither one came from the other — they are sisters, not parent and child. Both drifted, from something older that was neither.

But the third sister is standing exactly on the hinge. [sy: חֲפַט] does not mean wanting, and it does not mean guarding. It means applying yourself to something — being intent, taking pains, being diligent about it.

There is the whole story. The parent meant something like to fix your attention on a thing. Hebrew carried that inward, to the heart — what you are intent on is what you want, and you get [he: חֵפֶץ], desire. Arabic carried it outward, to the hands — what you are intent on is what you keep, and you get [ar: حِفْظ], guarding and preserving. Aramaic simply stayed where it started.

And you do not actually need Aramaic to find the feeling inside your own word, because Arabic kept it too. [ar: حَفيظة] is not a filing cabinet — it is zeal, ardour, caring fiercely about what is yours. [ar: حافَظَ عَلى الصَّلاة] is not guarding an object; it is sustained intent toward something you value. That is the Hebrew side of the meaning, still alive in your mouth.

Eight. What the line actually says.

So when the prayer says [he: לִמְחוֹז חֶפְצֵנוּ], it is not asking vaguely for a nice destination. [he: מְחוֹז] is the harbour you take hold of — from [he: אָחַז], your [ar: أَخَذَ]. And [he: חֶפְצֵנוּ] is the thing we are intent upon. The haven we are set on.

And one last thing to leave you with. Set the two inflected words beside each other — [he: חֶפְצֵנוּ] and [ar: حِفْظُنا]. Three consonants in the same order, the same collapse of the noun's vowels before the ending, the same possessive suffix, the same shape in the mouth. Whatever happened to the meaning, the grammar of those two words is one grammar. Three sisters, one root, three thousand years — and a [sy: טֵית] in the middle sister that gave the whole thing away.
