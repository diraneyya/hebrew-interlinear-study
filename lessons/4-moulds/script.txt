Three. What happens when the dual leans on another word.

Six. The mould works even when the root is a stranger.

Back to the mould itself, because it does something you will use for the rest of your life in this language.

Two words sit in line four that have never been explained to you, because their roots are genuinely not yours. There is no Arabic cousin. I am not going to invent one.

But look at their shape. An o, then an e — the same as [he: רוֹאֶה] and [he: שׁוֹמֵעַ]. They are in the doer-mould. So whatever the root means, the word means the one who does it.

Eight. Where your two phrases sit in your own prayer.

Now put both of the things you built tonight beside [ar: دُعاءُ السَّفَر], because that is where they belong.

Yours says [ar: أَعوذُ بِكَ مِنْ وَعْثاءِ السَّفَرِ] — take me out of what the road does to a man. And when you halt somewhere on the way you say [ar: أَعوذُ بِكَلِماتِ اللهِ التَّامّاتِ مِنْ شَرِّ ما خَلَق] — from the evil of everything He made. That is the same list the Hebrew spells out one item at a time.

Both hang on one letter: your [ar: مِنْ], their [he: מִ]. Hear the pair. [ar: مِنْ وَعْثاءِ السَّفَر] — [he: מִכַּף כָּל אוֹיֵב.]

The second one is harder to pair, and I will be straight with you: [ar: دُعاءُ السَّفَر] has no line for it. The nearest thing in your own book is what was said to Musa — [ar: وَأَلْقَيْتُ عَلَيْكَ مَحَبَّةً مِنّي] — I cast upon you love from Me, so that everyone who looked at him loved him.

That is precisely what the Hebrew traveller asks for: not safety, but to be looked at kindly by whoever meets him on the road. Same request, one carried in a verse, one in a prayer.
